# How I chose my career

*A few people have asked me how I ended up doing what I do. This is the honest version of the reasoning, holes included, so I have something to point at.*

Everything downstream follows from two beliefs.

The first is that technical AI safety is the most important thing I could be working on. I won't argue for it here; it's the premise.

The second is that within AI safety, I should do the thing that fits me best. "Work on AI safety" alone doesn't pick out a role. There are many, and they suit different people.

## What fits me

I think my superpower is noticing problems and working out what should be done about them. I generate a very large volume of ideas. I act on far fewer than 10% of them — maybe under 1%.

That ratio is the central fact about my career. If most of what I produce is proposals I'll never execute myself, then the leverage is in positions where I can say "I think this is worth doing, let's do it" and have other people run with it. So I've gravitated toward roles that allow that.

When I was mostly doing individual research, the same trait showed up as research taste: knowing what's interesting, then working on it for a long time. But taste on an IC track gets exercised once every three to six months — that's the cycle of picking a project and finishing it. The rest of the time it sits idle.

## Two experiences that set the constraints

**MATS with Owain Evans.** Relative to my seniority, I was mismatched. The stream wanted a cracked IC who could execute on other people's ideas. I already had my hands full acting on my own, on top of being involved in the group's projects. There was no protected time to sit back and ask "what do I think matters?" or "should I write this up?" That was stressful, and I burned out a bit.

**Five-week sprints.** I'd had a similar experience earlier in Neel Nanda's MATS stream. The five-week format means you spend your whole life grinding on one project. It's very stressful. Doing it twice convinced me that I want a job with real work-life balance, and I don't want to burn out like that again.

Those two experiences also settled some things I *won't* do. I don't want to do Anthropic Fellows: it's the sprint shape again, and I expect it to be a heavy-burnout experience. And I don't want to work at Anthropic. Inside a large lab I'd be a cog in the machine — I wouldn't be able to say "here's what I think we should do" and get buy-in for it. My opinion would mostly be ignored. I'd rather be independent than a cog.

## Independence as a preference, not a conclusion

I've been lucky here. People I respect — Richard Ngo, Geoffrey Irving — have actually been inside labs, and can argue from experience about the benefits of being a third party. Most people have to convince themselves to leave the labs by reasoning. For me it's simpler: being independent of the labs is what I enjoy. Starting up, doing my own thing, working with a small team.

There's an argument that this is also the right call. But I want to be clear that it isn't why I made it.

## The holes I know about

- The sprint evidence is mostly about sprints, not about IC work in general. "I burn out under five-week deadlines on someone else's agenda" is a narrower claim than "IC roles don't fit me".
- I've never worked inside a lab. "My opinion would be ignored" is a prior, not an observation.
- A preference that happens to match my conclusion is exactly where motivated reasoning hides. The strongest case against independence — losing internal discussion bandwidth and proximity to the frontier — comes from the same ex-lab people I cited above, and I haven't engaged with it here.

None of that has changed the decision. But if you're using this as a template, weight the preference part honestly.
